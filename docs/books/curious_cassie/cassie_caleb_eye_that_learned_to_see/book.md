## 🔬 Meet the Real Scientist: Dr Fei-Fei Li

**Computer Scientist, AI Pioneer, and Champion of Human-Centred Technology** · *1976 – present*

> She taught computers how to see the world by realising they needed to learn the way babies do — from millions of patient, labelled examples.

Dr Fei-Fei Li is one of the most important computer scientists alive today. People often call her the 'Godmother of AI' because of the work she did to help computers learn how to see.

Fei-Fei was born in Beijing, China, in 1976 and grew up in a busy city called Chengdu. She was a curious child who loved reading, asking questions, and outperforming the boys in her maths class — even when teachers thought that was strange. When she was about fifteen, her family did something brave: they moved across the world to a small town in New Jersey, in America, so Fei-Fei could go to a better school. She didn't speak a word of English when she arrived. Her parents opened a small dry-cleaning shop to support the family, and Fei-Fei helped run it — answering the phones, sorting shirts, and learning English by reading the labels on clothing tags. A kind maths teacher named Mr Bob Sabella saw how curious she was and helped her with books and friendship. He even helped her parents buy the dry-cleaning shop.

Fei-Fei studied so hard she won a full scholarship to Princeton University, where she studied physics. Then she went to the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) for her PhD, where she became fascinated by a big question: why were computers so bad at recognising the things in pictures? People had been trying to fix this for years by writing cleverer and cleverer computer programs, but Fei-Fei had a different idea. She noticed that babies learn to see by looking at thousands and thousands of different things — faces, spoons, dogs, clouds, the patterns on curtains — every single day, for years. What if computers needed the same? What if the problem wasn't the program, but the lack of examples?

So Fei-Fei and her team built **ImageNet** — a gigantic collection of over fourteen million labelled photographs, organised into more than twenty thousand kinds of thing. It took years of work, with help from tens of thousands of people around the world. When ImageNet was finally given to the world's computers, something incredible happened: machines suddenly started getting much, much better at seeing. The 'AI revolution' that powers your phone, self-driving cars, and modern medical scanners was sparked, in large part, by Fei-Fei's audacious idea.

Today, Dr Li is a professor at Stanford University. She is the co-director of the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, which works to make sure AI is built to help people, not harm them. She co-founded a charity called AI4ALL that invites girls, children of colour, and children from low-income families to learn about AI, so that the future of these powerful machines belongs to everyone — not just a few. She has written a beautiful memoir about her life called *The Worlds I See*. In 2025, she received the Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering, one of the highest honours in the world for an inventor. And she still asks audacious questions every day.

**Amazing Facts:**

* Fei-Fei moved from China to America when she was about fifteen and didn't speak any English when she arrived — she learned by reading clothing labels, cereal boxes, and street signs.
* She ran her family's dry-cleaning shop for seven years, including all the way through her studies at Princeton University. She liked to joke that she was 'the CEO'.
* Her favourite subject in high school wasn't science — it was American history. She especially loved learning about Benjamin Franklin.
* She had a maths teacher called Mr Sabella who believed in her so much that he and his wife helped her parents buy the dry-cleaning shop.
* ImageNet, the dataset she built, has more than 14 million labelled pictures organised into over 20,000 different categories. If you tried to look at one picture every second, it would take you more than five months without sleeping to look at them all.
* She co-founded AI4ALL in 2017 to invite girls, children of colour, and other under-represented kids into the world of artificial intelligence.
* In 2025 she won the Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering — one of the world's top prizes for inventors — for her work on ImageNet.
* She wrote a memoir called *The Worlds I See: Curiosity, Exploration, and Discovery at the Dawn of AI*.
* She believes that AI should be 'human-centred' — meaning it should be built carefully and kindly, by lots of different kinds of people, to help everybody.
* Today she also co-founded a company called World Labs that is teaching computers not just to see flat pictures, but to understand three-dimensional spaces.

**Why She Inspires Us:**

Dr Fei-Fei Li's story shows that big discoveries can begin in very ordinary places — even behind the counter of a small dry-cleaning shop. She reminds us that curiosity is a kind of superpower, and that the best ideas often come from paying close attention to something everyone else overlooks — like the way a baby learns. She is also a beautiful example of a scientist who cares not just about making technology *smart*, but about making it *kind* and *fair*. She has spent her career making sure that the future of AI is shaped by lots of different people from lots of different places. Most of all, she gives every child permission to ask audacious questions — the kind that feel too big or too wild — and to keep asking them, no matter where they're from or what language they first learned to speak.

## 🧪 Try It Yourself: The Human Pixel-Bot — How to Think Like a Computer

**The Big Idea:** Computers don't see whole pictures the way we do. They see a grid of tiny coloured dots (pixels) and rely on labels — names humans give them — to guess what each picture shows. In this experiment, you'll become a 'pixel-bot' and discover for yourself why teaching a computer to see is harder than it sounds.

**What You'll Need:**

* A piece of graph paper, or a plain sheet with a grid of squares drawn in pencil (at least 10 squares wide and 10 squares tall)
* A second blank piece of graph paper of the same size
* Coloured markers, crayons, or pencils — at least four different colours
* A 'Secret Image' card prepared by an adult or older sibling (a very simple shape — a heart, a smiley face, a star, or the letter 'C' — coloured in on one grid)
* A small mirror, or you can simply step far back and squint
* A notebook to record what you noticed

**What To Do:**

1. Adult or helper: On one piece of graph paper, draw a simple shape by colouring in only certain squares to make a clear picture (for example, a red heart on a white background). This is the 'Secret Image'. Do not show it to the child yet.
2. Child: You are now the 'Pixel-Bot'. Sit at the table with your blank grid in front of you and the Secret Image hidden by a folder or book.
3. Helper: Read the Secret Image to the child *one square at a time*, going row by row from left to right, like reading a book. Say only the colour of each square. For example: 'Row one: white, white, red, red, white, white. Row two: white, red, red, red, red, white...' and so on.
4. Child (Pixel-Bot): As each colour is called, fill in the matching square on your own blank grid. You may not look at the Secret Image at any point. You can only listen and colour.
5. When the grid is full, hold your finished grid up close to your face. What does it look like? Can you tell what the picture is when your eyes are right on it?
6. Now hold the grid at arm's length, or look at it in a mirror, or squint your eyes so that the squares blur together. What do you see now?
7. Try to *label* the picture. Say out loud: 'I think this is a...' and write your guess in your notebook.
8. Bonus round: Now switch roles. The child draws a new Secret Image and reads it out to the helper, one square at a time. See if your helper can guess the label.
9. Extra challenge: Try the same experiment with a really tricky picture — for example, a picture of a dog made of only ten coloured squares. Was it harder? Why?

**What To Watch For:** Notice how, when your eyes are very close to the page, the image looks like a confusing soup of coloured squares — this is what a computer 'sees' all the time. It's just a list of numbers (or colours) one after another. The picture only becomes a recognisable thing when the dots blur together and your brain spots the pattern. Now imagine doing this with millions of squares, like real computers do. The amazing part is that computers can't squint or step back — so to help them, scientists like Dr Fei-Fei Li give them a 'label' (the name of the picture) next to many, many examples, so the computer slowly learns which patterns of dots go with which name. Thinking question: if you only ever showed the computer pictures of red hearts, what would it guess if you suddenly gave it a green star?

!!! warning "Safety First"
    Be careful when handling a mirror or any glass — keep it away from the edge of the table so it can't fall. Tidy up your markers when you're done so nobody slips on a stray crayon.

## 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 For Parents & Educators

**Why this book matters now.** This book introduces children aged 5–10 to one of the most important ideas of their lifetime: that artificial intelligence isn't magic, isn't a brain, and isn't really 'thinking' the way they are. It's a pattern-finder taught by people. Helping a child grasp this — gently, without fearmongering — is one of the most empowering gifts you can give them. A child who understands that AI is *taught* understands two crucial things at once: that it can be wrong (because its teachers were limited or careless), and that it can be improved (because its teachers can choose differently). This is the foundation of healthy AI literacy.

**How to use the book together.** As you read, encourage your child to ask 'How does it know?' about anything around them — the photo app, the voice assistant, the recommendation feed on a tablet. The question itself is the lesson. When they get an answer (or a guess), follow up with 'And how does *that* know?' — the curiosity ladder is endless and rewarding. Pay special attention to Chapters 5 and 7, where the book gently introduces the idea that AI's mistakes are echoes of *what its teachers chose to show it*. This is the kid-friendly seed of bias, fairness, and inclusion in machine learning. You don't need to use those words; the story does the work. Afterwards, you might ask your child whether something from your own home or family — a food, a face, a name, a story — would have been in the computer's 'scrapbook'. Notice what they say. That noticing is the beginning of digital citizenship.

**Conversations this book opens up.** Children may come away with new questions — about their phones, about screens, about what's true online. Use the book as a springboard. Some questions worth exploring together: *Who decides what the computer learns? Who is missing from its pictures? When the computer is wrong, who fixes it? Could you be one of the people who builds it?* You don't need answers. You only need to take the questions seriously. Like Dr Fei-Fei Li, the best AI educators in your child's life will be the grown-ups who treat audacious questions as gifts — not nuisances. We hope this book becomes the start of many such conversations in your family.
